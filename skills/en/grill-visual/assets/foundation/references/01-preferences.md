# 01 Preferences and principles

Abstract rules that apply to every HTML page: preferences and principles for readable pages.

## Pictures are the protagonist of a document

- Make the picture the protagonist and add a short line. Fold code, tables and long explanations into "Details".
- Do not make boxes of text, boxes connected by arrows, or blocks of code the protagonist.
  Think first of a scene (a picture of someone doing something), a realistic screen mock-up or a visual analogy.
- Use one means of expression per diagram.


## Analogies are fine, but the content is the real thing with real names

- An analogy (a road, a gate, a barrier, a shelf) may be the protagonist. What rides on it is the real thing with real names.
  Examples: what travels the road is a PR card; the gate's lamp is CI's ✓ ✗; the destination is the main branch line; a table is a mock-up of a DB tool's table with real column names.
- Do not turn the content into analogies too (a word book for a dictionary, an envelope for an answer row). It becomes harder to picture.
- A close-up may be the real thing (a PR screen and so on). Choose whichever communicates better.
- When drawing a real screen as a made-up one, mark it "example".


## Draw down to the level of the steps. Show boundaries in the overview with areas

- Do not stop at an overview of a few boxes. Draw steps, human checkpoints, who does what, deliverables and loops back at the granularity of the source.
  Deciding the style does not decide the level of detail; make the content detailed separately.
- Separate the overview (one sheet) from close-ups. The overview has about four protagonists; detailed steps go to close-ups or tags.
- Name each area of the overview and show its boundary. Give important areas a thick frame. Put area backgrounds on the background layer.
- Tags that open on press are also diagrams, not tables of text. Where a person checks something, draw "from where to where they look" and why a person checks it.
- If the source has a table of steps, copy its numbers and names into the diagram as they are.


## Do not flow items in one row; nest them under headings

- Do not cram items of different kinds into one row. Make a tree of heading → content (nested) and show where each item begins and ends.
- Line things up horizontally only when comparing things of the same kind.


## Do not narrow the range of expression. Conceive each diagram anew

- Do not copy diagrams from examples or earlier documents. Consider two or three means of expression per issue, then choose. Do not repeat the same type of diagram on one page.
- Rules constrain only the default look (02) and breakage (05). What to draw and how is thought out each time.
- Use ways of seeing that only HTML has (press to dig in, switch to compare, step one frame at a time). If it would be the same on paper, HTML adds little.


## Choose one direction and stick to it

- Left to AI, the look drifts to the most common form. Choose one direction from the subject (02) and carry typeface, color, parts and diagrams through in that direction. Do not mix midway.


## Combinations to avoid

- Purple to blue-violet gradients.
- Off-white background with rust accents ("the Claude look", the most common default after purple).
- Large decorative numbers (01 / 02 / 03), meaningless emoji, stacked frames, gradients and shadows.
- Choose colors by deciding their roles from the subject, not from "tasteful defaults".
